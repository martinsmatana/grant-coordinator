Studio· Introduction

grant funding.

Grant Coordinator is a specialized internship position at the Smatana creative studio. The role focuses on preparing, submitting and implementing international grant applications — primarily from European programs such as Creative Europe MEDIA, Horizon Europe, Erasmus+ and Visegrad Fund, as well as from national funds in Estonia, Slovakia, Czechia and Germany.

The role is designed as a 12-month internship with the prospect of moving into a full-time position (Funding Coordinator or Development Producer). The Grant Coordinator works closely with the Creative Director, and on specific projects with an external EU funding consultant. A core part of the role is also skilled collaboration with AI agents (Claude Cowork, Claude Design, Lovable, n8n, Cursor), which take care of repetitive and monitoring tasks so the Grant Coordinator can focus on strategic, written and interpretive work.

Studio· Why grants

A strategic mechanism,
not just a financial tool.

Funding creative projects through European and international grants is not just a financial tool — it is a strategic mechanism that allows works and products to exist that would otherwise never be made on a purely commercial basis in Europe, or would only exist in a watered-down, compromised form.

Why these works would not exist otherwise

Without grant funding, auteur animated films, picture books in minority languages, transmedia IP rooted in local culture and edu-tech products for specific communities simply would not be financed. Investors and commercial studios look for scale and short-term ROI, which systematically excludes the very projects that have the highest cultural, educational and social value.

Grants act as the kind of capital that allows us to develop high-quality animation, properly validate educational products with teachers and pedagogues, build AI tools for children in an ethical way, and create work that still resonates years later.

Why this direction matters now

When children in Europe grow up in a digital environment where 95% of the content, AI tools and interactive experiences come from Disney, Netflix, TikTok, OpenAI and Synthesia, this shapes not only their visual taste but also their values, language, view of the world and connection to their own culture.

Creative Europe, Horizon Europe, Erasmus+ and CERV exist precisely to build the infrastructure for European-made alternatives — and every successfully funded work increases the chance that the next generation will actually have a choice.

Grants as a network

Grants also create cross-border collaborations that would not be commercially viable on their own, but which build the European creative ecosystem over time. These networks do not appear by chance or for market reasons; they exist because grant programmes require and fund them. From these networks come talent (mobility schemes, internships, Erasmus+), know-how (R&D consortia under Horizon), distribution infrastructure (Films on the Move) and institutional memory (Europeana, NEMO museum networks) that lasts long after individual projects end.

The moral case

Finally, in the specific case of AI, edu-tech and child-safe digital products, there is a direct moral case for public funding: these areas are too important to leave entirely to the market. AI products for children that comply with GDPR and the EU AI Act, that are linguistically and culturally adapted for small European markets, and that are designed with pedagogical integrity instead of maximising engagement metrics, will never be price-competitive against US Big Tech alternatives funded by advertising and data harvesting. Grants compensate for this market asymmetry and make it possible for European edu products to exist that parents, teachers and cultural institutions can use without an ethical compromise. This is not a subsidy in the negative sense — it is an investment in the public good, in the quality of childhood, in the democratic resilience of society, and in Europe having its own creative and technological voice in the 21st century.

That is why the work of our creative studio — which deliberately develops these intersections — is exactly the kind of activity European grant programmes were designed for. Funding through these programmes is not a side source of capital; it is a legitimate and strategically correct way of building a sustainable, value-driven and internationally competitive creative ecosystem.

Candidate profile
for the Grant Coordinator role.

Education and background

  • Currently enrolled as a university student, ideally in: European Studies, International Relations, Cultural Management, Film & Media Studies, Project Management, Business / Public Administration, Translation Studies or Law (especially European law).
  • Previous experience with European institutions is welcome (Erasmus stay, Model UN, student EU projects), as is work at a cultural institution, NGO or film/animation studio.
  • An academic interest in European integration, the creative industries and cultural policy is a plus — we are not looking for people who "just do what is asked", but for those who are genuinely interested in the topic.

Languages

English at a high level (C1 or above) — not just conversational, but able to read complex EU regulatory documents and write professional grant applications. This is a hard requirement.

Slovak as a native language, for working with national funds (AVF, FPU).

Any additional EU language (especially German, Estonian or French) is a strong plus.

Hard skills

  • Advanced writing — being able to write in a structured, persuasive and well-argued way, and to adjust tone of voice across registers (formal EU language vs. friendly communication with consortium partners).
  • Working with spreadsheets and budgets (Google Sheets, Excel) — being able to read and prepare budget structures, percentage calculations and timelines.
  • Comfort with digital tools — Notion, Google Workspace, ideally also Figma or another design tool.
  • Ability to learn fast and use AI tools competently (Claude, Cursor, Lovable, n8n) — prior experience is not required, but openness and quick adaptation are.

Soft skills

  • Systematic, attentive to detail — grant applications are more often rejected for small formal mistakes (wrong PDF format, a missing declaration, incorrect file naming) than for content.
  • Long-term persistence — between submission and evaluation results 4–9 months can pass; the role requires the ability to work without immediate feedback.
  • Discretion and reliability — the Grant Coordinator handles non-public strategic information, financial data of consortium partners and IP-protected materials.
  • Comfort working both alone and in a team — autonomously on individual tasks, but with the willingness to communicate continuously with the Creative Director, producer and external consultants.
  • Patience in dealing with institutions — EU and national fund administrators work at their own pace; their bureaucracy is a fact of life, not a glitch.

What the Grant Coordinator does NOT do

  • Does not make strategic decisions about which grants to pursue — they prepare the supporting materials and recommendations, but the final decision belongs to the Creative Director and the producer.
  • Does not submit applications independently — the final review and the actual submission via the F&T Portal or other systems is done by the Creative Director (for eligibility and producer credit reasons, especially in MEDIA calls).
  • Does not lead negotiations with donors and consortium partners — they prepare the materials, but strategic-level communication is led by the Creative Director or an external consultant.
  • Does not approve budgets and financial plans — they prepare the drafts and supporting materials; final approval is on the producer's side.

These boundaries are not limitations — they are protection. They protect the Grant Coordinator from responsibilities that belong to someone else, and the studio from eligibility risks that could otherwise jeopardise a successful application.

Role· Internship duties

An independent expert
function, not assistance.

The Grant Coordinator internship is built on a combination of systematic research, high-quality writing in English, coordination of several parallel projects and practical work with AI agents. This is not a classic assistant role — within the studio, the Grant Coordinator is an independent expert function that holds the entire international grant agenda together and pushes it forward in quality. The scope and character of the work reflect this: over 12 months the Grant Coordinator gradually learns the full cycle of European and national grant applications — from mapping calls to preparing applications and implementing successful projects — and at every stage works closely with the Creative Director (strategic decisions) and, on complex calls (Horizon Europe, Creative Europe Cooperation Projects), also with an external EU funding consultant.

Three main areas of work

On a daily and weekly level, this means three main areas of work.

01 · Monitoring

Monitoring and qualifying calls

Regularly checking outputs from AI agents, reading current call documents on EU programme portals, qualifying new calls against the Smatana roadmap, and proposing to the Creative Director which calls should move into active preparation.

02 · Application drafting

Preparing grant applications

For a specific call, the Grant Coordinator runs the full process — from a first feasibility report, through drafting the text sections of the application (project description, impact statement, work packages, dissemination and sustainability plan, budget rationale), to assembling all required annexes. Texts are written in English. The Grant Coordinator actively uses AI tools (Claude Projects for drafting, Claude Design for visual annexes, Lovable or v0 for interactive prototypes as annexes), but critically reviews and rewrites every AI-generated output so that the result reads as authored, strategic work — not boilerplate.

03 · Coordination

Coordination and communication

Preparing and sending status updates to consortium partners across countries, tracking deadlines, drafting replies for Creative Europe Desks and national funds, maintaining internal project documentation in Notion, and coordinating the application timeline within the team.

Reverse mapping and pre-application contact

The daily and weekly work also includes reverse mapping of grant options for specific projects — the opposite of the call monitoring process. For every new studio project (auteur film, transmedia IP, edu product, co-production), the Grant Coordinator prepares a structured overview of all grant programmes the project could potentially fit. This includes an estimate of chances, the potential funding amount, deadlines and the required partnerships. The overview is the basis for joint decision-making sessions with the Creative Director and the producer (usually monthly or at the start of each new project cycle), where it is decided which grant routes are activated for a given project and in what order. Another important task is direct contact with grant fund administrators — written and phone-based pre-application consultations with officers at the Creative Europe Desks (EE, SK, DE), EACEA, the Slovak Audiovisual Fund, FPU, the Estonian Film Institute and Erasmus+ national agencies. This covers questions about eligibility, scope, what types of expenditures and partnerships are acceptable, and follow-up communication after submission, including responding to evaluator clarification requests. This communication layer is often decisive for an application's success — administrators can quietly point you towards what really works in evaluation and what does not, and a well-run dialogue with an officer builds institutional memory for the studio that pays off in future evaluations.

Submission and follow-up

Submitting an application means filling in the official systems — the EU's Funding & Tenders Portal (mandatory registrations, PIC codes, ownership declarations, financial documents) for EU programmes, and national eGrant systems with their own logic and technical quirks for Estonian and Slovak funds. Some national funds (especially AVF, FPU and parts of Czech schemes) still require paper submission — printing, signing, sometimes notarisation, and physically sending in an official envelope. The Grant Coordinator handles this entire mechanical process, including the final check that the submitted materials match the call, monitoring delivery confirmations, and recording reference numbers. After submission, they track status in the relevant portals, monitor evaluation phases, prepare written responses to evaluator clarification requests (which usually require a reply within 7–10 working days), and, if the application is successful, prepare the studio for the negotiation phase that follows.

Monthly rhythm

On a monthly level, the work splits into a predictable agenda (weekly digests, monitoring, deadline tracking) and project peaks during the preparation of a specific application (typically 2–4 weeks of intense work before the submission deadline). During peaks the rhythm speeds up and more time flexibility is needed; between peaks the pace is calmer and the Grant Coordinator can focus on longer-term tasks — building the studio's evergreen project documentation (corporate dossiers, founder bio, master CVs, templates for recurring sections), tone-of-voice profiles for different donors, an internal library of successful applications as reference, and optimisation of AI workflow systems. Once a grant is awarded, the implementation and reporting agenda is added — coordinating expense records, drafting interim and final reports (with Claude Cowork helping to parse financial documentation), and preparing audit-ready files.

Time split

In rough numbers, this role represents an average of 15–25 hours of work per week (with flexibility during exam periods and pre-deadline peaks). About half of the time goes into application preparation, a quarter into monitoring and strategic work, and a quarter into implementation and reporting on running projects. Thanks to the AI infrastructure, which covers repetitive and monitoring tasks, the Grant Coordinator can focus on judgement-based, written and strategic work. That work is the core value of this role and also the fastest way to build expertise that is in short supply across Europe.

Role· Onboarding plan

A draft
onboarding plan.

The first 12 months are split into four phases that gradually move the Grant Coordinator from orientation, through assistance, to running their own grant applications. The point of this structure is that no one should ever have to handle a task on day one for which they do not yet have the context or tools. Each phase has clear expectations, clear outputs and a steadily growing degree of autonomy — the Grant Coordinator always knows where they stand and what is expected of them next.

Day 1 · Week 1

Kick-off + passive onboarding

The internship starts with a kick-off meeting with the Creative Director (a 90-minute online session), where the Grant Coordinator gets access to all systems (Notion, Gmail, Google Workspace, AI tools), is introduced to the team and the external collaborators, and receives a starter pack of reading materials (the studio's strategic grant report, an overview of projects in preparation, sample successful applications, brand documents). The first week is dedicated entirely to passive familiarisation — no pressure to produce real outputs, no deadlines. The aim is for the Grant Coordinator to enter the second week with a good understanding of the context and the first concrete questions for the Creative Director.

Month 1

Orientation and infrastructure

The first month is dedicated to a deep understanding of the context in which Smatana studio operates, and to practical familiarisation with the entire AI infrastructure. During this period, the Grant Coordinator reads the studio's strategic grant report (50+ verified grant programmes across the EU, EE, SK, CZ, DE and the Nordics), gets to know the main projects in preparation (auteur animation, transmedia IP, the Compactino edu product, co-productions), studies 3–5 sample successful applications (both ours and publicly available references), and learns the key tools: Notion as the central database for grants and projects, Claude Cowork as the main AI assistant, the European Commission's Funding & Tenders Portal, and the official websites of AVF, FPU, EFI, the Visegrad Fund and other national funds. This first month also includes setting up monitoring automations together with the admin team (n8n RSS feeds, Notion alerts, scheduled Cowork tasks) — so that the Grant Coordinator gets to know the AI infrastructure not just as a user, but as a co-architect. The concrete outputs of the first month are: an updated Notion grant database, a working weekly digest, and a first draft of a monthly strategic overview for the Creative Director.

Months 2 — 3

Assisting on the first application

During this period the Grant Coordinator joins an in-progress grant application under the direct guidance of the Creative Director, ideally on a smaller, well-scoped call. They work on specific delegated tasks — drafting individual sections, completing annexes, partner search, eligibility checks, preparing budget tables — and gradually get a feel for what the full preparation cycle looks like in practice. The Creative Director acts as a mentor here, gives feedback on every draft, and explains strategic decisions that would not be obvious from the grant documentation alone. This period also includes the first contact with the Creative Europe Desk Slovakia or Estonia (under CD's guidance), the first feasibility report prepared independently, and joining the actual submission via the F&T Portal or a national eGrant system. By the end of month 3, the Grant Coordinator should understand the full process from start to finish, have at least one fully submitted application as an assistant, and know where their strengths and weaknesses are.

Months 4 — 6

Running the first application independently

From month 4, the Grant Coordinator takes ownership of the first application that they run independently from mapping through to submission. The Creative Director remains as the final approver and strategic partner, but day-to-day operations, drafting, coordination and contact with fund administrators are now on the Grant Coordinator's side. In parallel, they typically also work in a supporting role on a second application (assisting on a more complex consortium project, e.g. Erasmus+ KA220 or a Creative Europe Cooperation), so they own a smaller application and co-create a larger one at the same time. Month 6 ends with a joint six-month review with the Creative Director, where the collaboration so far, the completed applications, the quality of outputs, and the direction for the second half of the internship are all assessed.

Months 7 — 12

Wider scope and specialisation

In the second half of the internship, the Grant Coordinator works on 3–4 parallel applications at different stages (mapping, drafting, submission, awaiting results, or implementing successfully awarded grants from the first half of the year). During this period, they gradually take ownership of a personal portfolio of grant programmes in which they become the studio's internal expert — for example national funds in SK + CZ, or the Erasmus+ + CERV cluster, or Nordic-Baltic schemes. Another task is to optimise AI workflow systems based on real experience from the first applications (better prompts for Claude, improved n8n automation flows, evergreen templates for repeated sections of applications), which directly raises the studio's efficiency for the years to come. The final month is dedicated to knowledge transfer and final review — either as a transition to long-term collaboration (Funding Coordinator or Development Producer), or as a handover to the next Grant Coordinator with full documentation.

This timeline is a framework, not a rigid plan — the actual progression can be influenced by external reality (newly published calls, results of previous applications, joining an unexpected consortium project), individual pace, and the Grant Coordinator's strengths. The principle, however, stays: each phase adds one layer of responsibility, but never in a way that puts the Grant Coordinator in front of a task without the necessary context. This structure is shaped by the experience of established European creative studios that have had this role figured out for a long time — and is set up so that even a first internship of this kind at Smatana ends with concrete, measurable results (number of applications prepared, value of contracted funding) and a real basis for long-term collaboration.

Role· Team and how we work together

Direct line,
async via Notion.

The Grant Coordinator works in a direct line with the Creative Director, who is the main strategic and content partner for the entire grant agenda — they approve which calls to pursue, finalise the key sections of applications, lead strategic communication with donors and consortium partners, and have the final word on every submission. Alongside the Creative Director, the Grant Coordinator relies on the admin team, which provides the technical and infrastructure layer (AI tools, Notion systems, automations, IT access). For more complex applications (especially Horizon Europe consortia, large Creative Europe Cooperation Projects, or a first MEDIA submission), an external EU funding consultant joins the process — either from the Creative Europe Desks network or from a specialised agency — bringing expertise on the specific call. Once a grant is awarded, an external accountant joins the team (handling accounting and tax aspects of the project, including financial statements for reports), and for grants above a certain financial threshold an external certified auditor as well (issuing the mandatory audit certificate before the final payment). The Grant Coordinator works with these external collaborators in a coordinated way — communication on specific projects always happens with the Creative Director in the loop, since the Creative Director remains the primary contact for strategic and financial decisions. The Grant Coordinator gets to know all external collaborators within the first month of onboarding. In these situations, the Grant Coordinator stays the main operational owner of the process, with the external consultant in an advisory role.

Working rhythm

The working rhythm is built on three regular contact points. Weekly check-ins with the Creative Director (typically 30–45 minutes online, in a fixed time slot) cover current applications, deadlines, AI agent outputs, open questions and next steps. Monthly strategic sessions with the Creative Director and the producer (60–90 minutes) focus on reverse mapping of grants for new projects, reviewing the past month, and planning the next one. Project peaks before deadlines are usually handled through more intense daily communication via Notion comments, with ad-hoc calls when needed. Outside these regular contact points, communication is asynchronous through Notion (operational tasks, drafts, comments) and Slack or email (quick questions, external communication). The Grant Coordinator keeps a weekly working log in Notion, available to the Creative Director at any time — this minimises the need for micromanagement and maximises transparency and mutual trust.

How we share decisions

The principle of the whole collaboration is simple: the Grant Coordinator carries operational responsibility, the Creative Director carries strategic and final responsibility. Whenever it is unclear who should decide, the rule is "if it affects the content of an application, the studio brand or relationships with partners, the Creative Director decides; if it is operational, technical or process-related, the Grant Coordinator decides". This split is not rigid — as the Grant Coordinator gains experience and trust, the line moves in their favour, and in the second half of the internship they typically also cover decisions that, in the first months, belonged exclusively to the Creative Director.

AI & processes· AI agents

AI handles volume,
the GC keeps judgement.

In the first phase of onboarding, the studio's admin team and the Grant Coordinator together set up a stack of AI agents and automations that will serve as the daily support infrastructure. The central tool is Claude Cowork — a desktop agent that can read local files, work with folders, control the browser and run multi-step tasks without constant supervision. Around it, the stack includes n8n or Make for tool-to-tool automation (RSS scraping, notifications, sync), Notion as the central database for grants and deadlines, Claude Design for visual annexes, Lovable or v0 by Vercel for fast interactive prototypes, and Cursor or Claude Code for any custom scripts. The point of this setup is for the AI agents to cover repetitive, time-consuming and monitoring tasks, while the Grant Coordinator holds the strategic, creative and interpretive decisions — where the real value is created.

Mapping and monitoring calls

In practice it works like this. Mapping and monitoring grant calls is handled by an n8n automation with RSS feeds and email parsers from 15–20 official sources (the F&T Portal, EACEA, Creative Europe Desks, AVF, FPU, EFI, Visegrad, Nordic Culture Point and private foundations). New calls are written automatically into a Notion database called "Grants" with basic metadata. Claude Cowork runs as a scheduled task every Monday morning — it goes through the new entries, compares them with the Smatana strategic roadmap, and produces a one-page weekly digest with recommendations: "worth attention / monitor / not relevant". The Grant Coordinator's job at this stage is to qualify the agent's output with expert judgement, add a context note to each call, identify consortium opportunities, and suggest to the Creative Director which calls should move into active preparation.

Preparing a specific application

For a specific application that the studio decides to pursue, the process happens on two levels. Claude Cowork downloads and parses the call document (typically a 30–80 page PDF in EU regulatory language), produces an initial eligibility checklist, identifies the evaluation criteria, and extracts the required annexes. Claude Projects holds the application context throughout the whole preparation — the call document, previous successful applications as reference, brand documents, project treatments. On this basis, Claude Sonnet (or Opus) generates first drafts of the text sections — project description, impact statement, work packages, dissemination plan, sustainability plan — always against a brief from the Grant Coordinator. For the visual side (mood boards, character bible, infographics, pitch deck as annex), Claude Design is used with PDF/PPTX export, and Figma for the final touch-up. If the application has a tech component, Lovable or v0 by Vercel can build a working interactive prototype within hours, which is then attached as an annex — a real differentiator against traditional applicants who attach static mockups. The Grant Coordinator's job is to lead this whole process: prepare briefs for the AI tools, prompt them well, critically read and rewrite generated drafts (AI texts have to be reworked thoroughly by a human so they read as authored, strategic work, not as boilerplate), keep sections internally consistent, complete the required annexes, and coordinate communication with consortium partners.

After submission

After submission, agent support continues. Before submission, Claude Cowork runs a compliance audit — it checks the format of annexes, naming conventions and technical eligibility conditions. After submission, it creates a Notion record with all metadata and schedules follow-up tasks. When an application is approved, the agent parses the Grant Agreement (50–100 pages) and extracts the key obligations, deadlines and reporting requirements into an operational Notion page for the team. During implementation, it organises the folder structure, categorises documents (invoices, timesheets, contracts) by work package, and prepares first drafts of interim and final reports from the metadata in Notion. The Grant Coordinator's job is the final submission of the application, leading negotiations with donors, strategic decisions about budget reallocation during implementation, and communication with consortium partners — everything that requires human judgement, relationships and political sensitivity.

Email Agent

Part of the studio's wider AI infrastructure is also an Email Agent — an autonomous system that processes emails in both company inboxes overnight (classification, drafting replies, urgent push notifications via Telegram, and a morning interactive dashboard) and saves the Creative Director 5–15 hours a week. For the Grant Coordinator this means two practical things. First, all communication with donors, evaluators and consortium partners goes through the same agent, which keeps a context memory per recipient — so reply drafts for the Creative Europe Desk Estonia, an EACEA officer or a German co-production partner are automatically prepared in the right register and tone. Second, the Grant Coordinator gets prioritised notifications for urgent grant emails (clarification requests, GA negotiations, deadline announcements), so no time-sensitive communication slips through — which, in EU grant work where a 24-hour response window can determine eligibility, is critical.

Architecture principle

The principle of the whole architecture is simple: AI agents provide volume, repeatability and speed; the Grant Coordinator provides judgement, quality and strategy. Thanks to this split, one Grant Coordinator with proper AI support can realistically cover work that would traditionally require 2–3 full-time people — and along the way they gain practical mastery of the most advanced AI tools of 2026 (Claude Cowork, Claude Design, n8n, Lovable, Cursor) in a real commercial context. This is a quiet but very valuable layer of added value for their future career. The specific automation setup, prompt templates and workflow are fine-tuned iteratively during the internship, based on real experience from the first applications submitted — we expect the first three months to be a period of joint setup of the infrastructure between the Grant Coordinator, the Creative Director and the admin team.

AI & processes· Post-grant work

After signature, a
whole new layer of work opens up.

Once a Grant Agreement is signed, a whole new layer of work opens up that is often comparable in volume to the application itself — project implementation, financial management, documentation, reporting, audit and communication with the donor. In practice this work runs for 12 to 48 months depending on the programme, and it requires coordinated work by several roles in the studio. To prevent overload of any one person and to avoid duplicated responsibilities, post-grant activities at Smatana are split between five roles.

Grant Coordinator in the post-grant phase

In the post-grant phase, the Grant Coordinator covers all activities related to the documentation, reporting, dissemination and compliance layers of the project. Concretely, this means: gathering and organising all contractual documents, invoices, timesheets and receipts in shared cloud storage in the structure required by auditors and donors; maintaining the deliverables register and tracking that they are met; drafting interim and final reports (text, structure, narrative, impact metrics); checking that every public output of the project carries the required EU logo and the prescribed funding statements; collecting evidence of dissemination activities (screenshots, attendance lists, photo documentation, media mentions); consolidating partner reports in consortium projects; operational communication with the donor's officer for questions, follow-ups and clarifications; and full preparation of an audit-ready file that can be handed to an auditor at any moment. If the grant requires a formal change request (extension, partner replacement, budget reallocation above the allowed threshold), the Grant Coordinator prepares the materials, but the actual submission and communication is led by the Creative Director.

Creative Director and producer

The Creative Director and the producer hold the post-grant phase at the strategic and financial level. They approve every invoice and payment to freelancers, suppliers and partners; they authorise the actual bank payments; they lead negotiations with consortium partners in case of disputes, scope changes or problems with deliverables; they decide on budget reallocation across work packages; they sign all formal outputs to the donor and act as the donor's main contact in strategic matters. This split protects both the studio and the Grant Coordinator — financial and relational decisions have consequences that belong to people with contractual responsibility to the donor and producer credit on the project.

External accountant and auditor

An external accountant or accounting firm handles the classic accounting and tax layer of the project — booking each invoice and payment in the studio's accounting system, tax matters (VAT, income tax, cross-border tax on foreign payments), and especially the financial part of the reports in the structure required by the donor (the financial statement that is part of the interim and final report). This is a specialist competence the Grant Coordinator does not take on — partnering with an external accountant is one of the reasons the studio can implement grant projects to a high standard without overloading its internal team. For grants above a certain financial threshold (typically €325,000 in Horizon Europe, similar for larger MEDIA calls), an external certified auditor also comes in, issuing the mandatory audit certificate before the donor's final payment.

Studio Project Manager

The studio's Project Manager (a separate internship position running in parallel with the Grant Coordinator) holds the operational layer of the project itself — issuing contracts to freelancers and suppliers, communication around payment milestones with collaborators, purchasing materials and software for the project, coordinating production deadlines, and overall organisation of the shared drives. The Project Manager and the Grant Coordinator work as two parallel streams — one runs the project inwards (towards the team and production), the other outwards (towards the donor and compliance). Their agendas meet at several points (contracts, timesheets, expense records), but they are clearly separated: the Project Manager makes sure the work is happening, the Grant Coordinator makes sure it is properly documented for the donor and audit.

Practical principle

The practical principle behind this split is that the Grant Coordinator is not an accountant, not a financial manager, and not a producer — they are a specialist in grant process and compliance, who keeps the studio in line with donor requirements and makes sure every awarded grant is fully and correctly documented, accounted for (together with the accountant), and closed. At the start of every project's post-grant phase, the Creative Director, Grant Coordinator, Project Manager and external accountant hold a kick-off meeting where they set out, for that specific grant, who does what, what the reporting deadlines are, which work packages and deliverables are in play, and what the rhythm of monthly check-ins during implementation will be. This makes sure no document, invoice or deliverable falls through the cracks — and that the Grant Coordinator works in a clearly bounded but fully owned zone where they can grow professionally without taking on responsibilities they are not authorised or trained for.

AI & processes· Confidentiality, NDA and ethics

NDA, sensitive data
and the ethics of AI tools.

NDA and confidential information

The Grant Coordinator works with information that is strategically and financially sensitive — internal financial data of the studio, non-public treatments and concepts of upcoming projects, contacts and conditions of co-production partners, IP-protected materials, draft versions of grant applications before official submission, and, for awarded grants, documentation that falls under donor-specific confidentiality rules (especially in Horizon Europe consortia and Creative Europe Cooperation Projects). For this reason, joining the role includes signing a non-disclosure agreement (NDA) that applies during the entire collaboration and standardly for two years afterwards. The NDA covers all non-public information the Grant Coordinator comes into contact with, and explicitly extends to materials of consortium partners, towards whom the studio carries its own confidentiality obligation.

Practical rules

Day-to-day, a few practical rules apply. No studio, client, co-production partner document, or grant application draft may be shared with third parties without explicit approval from the studio leadership. Public communication about projects the Grant Coordinator works on (social media, LinkedIn, portfolio, academic work) is only allowed by agreement and typically only after the project has been officially announced. Communication with donors, evaluators or consortium partners always happens from company contacts and in coordination with the Creative Director — no representation of the studio in external communication without the CD's awareness.

AI tool ethics

Working with AI tools is a separate category that requires its own ethics and discipline. Cloud AI services (Claude, Cursor, Lovable, n8n cloud) process data on external servers — therefore the rule is: confidential financial data, IP-protected scripts, materials of consortium partners and personal data of third parties must not be uploaded into any AI tool, unless the specific enterprise plan with data residency guarantees allows it (which is checked separately for each tool). For such materials, either the studio's local AI infrastructure is used (Ollama on a workstation), or AI assistance is bypassed entirely. Another rule: AI-generated text for grant applications must always be reviewed and rewritten by the Grant Coordinator — no draft is submitted as a raw AI output. This is also a requirement of most EU programmes, which in recent years have started to explicitly require declarations on AI use and to exclude mechanically generated content. The final rule is not to use other applicants' submissions as templates — copying passages from publicly available successful applications by other studios is both an ethical and a process risk (it can be detected by evaluators who read hundreds of applications a year, and can endanger the studio's eligibility in future calls).

Infrastructure of trust

These rules are not a formality — they are the infrastructure of trust on which the whole collaboration rests. The Grant Coordinator is protected by them as much as the studio is, because work with sensitive information in EU grant settings is professional and reputational capital that is built up over years and can be lost in a single moment.

How the bonus is calculated

Co-financing from the studio's own funds, in-kind contributions, tax reliefs and cash rebates are not counted in the bonus base, because these are not grants in the strict sense — they are own capital or automatic tax mechanisms. In consortium projects (Erasmus+, Horizon, Cooperation Projects), the bonus is calculated only on the part of the grant that actually goes to our studio, not on the total consortium amount. For example, an Erasmus+ KA220 grant of €250,000 split between three partners, where €80,000 goes to our studio, gives a bonus calculated on €80,000 (which at 3% is €2,400). If we are the consortium coordinator and the funds first arrive in full to us before being passed on to partners, the bonus is calculated on the final amount that remains with us after redistribution.

Payment tranches and conditions

The bonus is paid in two tranches: 50% after signing the Grant Agreement (the funds are contractually secured) and 50% after we receive the first payment from the donor (the funds actually arrive in the studio's account). This keeps the system cash-flow safe for the studio and transparent for the Grant Coordinator. For multi-stage grant schemes (for example development → production → distribution), the bonus is tied to the specific submitted application that the Grant Coordinator actually worked on, not to the whole project lifecycle. So if the Grant Coordinator helped prepare a development application (€30,000), but the follow-up production application (€200,000) was prepared by another team member or external consultant, the bonus is paid only on the development part. If, after the final audit, the donor reclaims part of the funds (which can happen up to two years after project end, especially under Horizon Europe and Cooperation Projects), the already-paid bonus is reduced proportionally. To qualify for a bonus, the Grant Coordinator's contribution to the application must be clearly documented in the contract — typically at least 30% of the drafted text plus completion of annexes. If several Grant Coordinators work on the same application, the bonus is split proportionally based on their level of involvement, as confirmed by the Creative Director. With 2–3 successful grants of €200,000 or more over a 12-month internship, we are looking at a bonus range of €8,000–€22,000 — a strong incentive to work on applications carefully and well, without creating a fixed financial burden for the studio when an application does not go through.

A strategic approach to multi-source funding

One project can be submitted to several calls — for projects with a European dimension, multi-source funding (so-called stacking or co-financing) is normal practice. Using several grant programmes and funds to finance the same project concept is a standard and highly efficient model in European funding. The strategy is based on the principle of getting the maximum out of one strong concept through targeted adaptation.

The "Master project" concept and modular adaptation

The basis of successful submission is a comprehensive document (the Master project) that contains all the key aspects: the AI technical solution, the pedagogical method, and the creative vision. This document serves as the source base for individual calls, where the emphasis is adjusted to fit each call's specific focus.

From the perspective of time investment vs. potential return, this model is the most effective way to prepare projects. Tying the reward to the success of approved applications makes this method especially advantageous for the following reasons:

  • Risk diversification — Submitting one adapted project to 3–5 different funds statistically multiplies the chance that at least one application is approved.
  • Time optimisation — The initial investment in a high-quality Master project allows subsequent adaptations at a fraction of the time (about 20% of the original effort).
  • Cumulative returns — When several programmes provide funding in parallel, the bonuses stack up, creating a synergistic effect from working on a single shared concept.

Terms· Career growth and review

An open
working conversation.

The Grant Coordinator internship is primarily designed as the first step of a long-term collaboration, not as a one-off 12-month engagement. For this reason, the internship includes structured review rituals that allow both sides to keep checking whether the collaboration still makes sense and in what form. A review is not a formal HR audit — it is an open working conversation between the Grant Coordinator and the Creative Director, aimed at naming strengths, identifying room to grow, and calibrating the next direction.

M1 · One-month review

A binary question

The first milestone is the one-month review (at the end of month 1), which closes the first trial month of collaboration. This milestone is binary — its purpose is to answer the basic question of whether the collaboration makes sense for both sides. It looks at first impressions of the working dynamic, fit between working styles, the Grant Coordinator's ability to navigate the studio's specific context and AI-first methodology, and the clarity with which the Creative Director assigns tasks. If both sides confirm a mutual interest in continuing, the internship moves smoothly into the full 12-month phase. If not, the collaboration ends cleanly without further obligations.

M3 · Three-month review

Closes the initial assistance phase

The second milestone is the three-month review (at the end of month 3), which closes the initial phase of assisting on a first application. It includes a brief assessment of output quality, understanding of processes, communication dynamics, and first experiences with AI tools. At this point, the decision is made on whether and to what extent the Grant Coordinator takes independent ownership of their own application in the following months.

M6 · Six-month review

The main milestone

The third and main milestone is the six-month review, which evaluates the entire first half of the collaboration — the number of applications prepared and submitted, draft quality, ability to work independently, quality of communication with fund administrators and consortium partners, and contribution to optimising the studio's AI workflow systems.

M12 · Final review

Opens the question of long-term collaboration

The fourth and final milestone is the 12-month review, which closes the internship and opens the question of long-term collaboration.

Four practical evaluation layers

The review looks at four practical layers: operational reliability (deadlines, accuracy, completion of annexes, command of systems), quality of written outputs (application drafts, communication with donors, language level), strategic orientation (ability to qualify calls, propose prioritisation, understand project context), and soft skills (proactivity, discretion, ability to work in a team and alone, ethics around AI use). Beyond hard KPIs, measurable outcomes are also taken into account — number of applications prepared, value of contracted funding actually paid out to the studio, and the quality of evergreen documentation that remains valuable for future years.

Future paths

After successfully completing the 12-month internship, several natural paths exist for further growth. The most direct is a transition to a full-time Funding Coordinator or Development Producer role at Smatana, where the Grant Coordinator becomes the in-house expert for the entire international grant agenda. The second option is a specialised part-time collaboration alongside further studies or other professional activities — especially for those who want to develop their career in another field in parallel, but stay connected to grant work as an external expert. The third option is a recommendation into the wider ecosystem — Smatana actively collaborates with other institutions, and a Grant Coordinator with proven results has a real chance of placement in either an artistic or commercial environment. Across all three paths, ongoing collaboration with our studio remains an open possibility, because well-functioning relationships in EU grant work are a long-term value for both sides.

Certificate and reference

Whichever of these paths the Grant Coordinator takes after the internship, on completion they receive an official certificate for completing the 12-month Grant Coordinator internship (in both Slovak and English versions), and a personal reference from the Creative Director that lists the specific applications prepared, the value of contracted funding, and the key skills developed during the internship. This package is designed to be immediately usable when applying for positions in EU institutions, specialised agencies, or other European creative studios.

FAQ· Frequently asked questions

FAQ —
frequently asked questions.

Can I do the internship alongside another job or another internship?+
Yes. The Grant Coordinator role is designed as a part-time agenda (an average of 15–25 hours per week with time flexibility) and can be combined with another job, a parallel internship, or a small business run on the side. The only things that need to align are the weekly check-ins with the Creative Director and the monthly strategic sessions — these have fixed time slots; the rest of the work is asynchronous and flexible.
Can I do the internship during Erasmus or from another country?+
Yes. The whole role is remote-friendly, and Smatana works with a team of freelancers across Europe. If the Grant Coordinator goes on Erasmus during the internship or moves location, the work continues without changes — the weekly check-ins and monthly sessions just happen online. For national funds that occasionally still require paper submission, the postal handling is taken care of operationally within the team.
What if after 2–3 months I find out it's not for me?+
The internship has a built-in three-month review precisely so that both sides can speak openly about whether the collaboration should continue. If at this point either side concludes the fit is not right, ending the internship is a fair and transparent option with no negative consequences for the candidate — on the contrary, the time already spent stays as a valid CV entry, with a written reference from the Creative Director and an official confirmation of the part of the internship completed (proportional to the time worked).
What if no grant is awarded over 12 months?+
This is a realistic scenario that needs to be named directly. EU and national grant programmes typically have 15–35% success rates depending on the programme, which means that even a well-prepared application can be rejected for reasons unrelated to the Grant Coordinator's work (political priorities, that year's competition, formal coincidences in the evaluation process). The internship is therefore designed as unpaid — there is no fixed monthly stipend. The bonus mechanism (3% of approved grant amounts) is the only financial component and is tied strictly to actual success. Even in a scenario where no grant comes through over 12 months, the Grant Coordinator leaves with a measurable work history (number of applications prepared and submitted, tools mastered, EU institutional contacts) that is professional capital in its own right — because the strategic value of this internship is not a monthly salary, but skills, references and contacts that nowhere else can be obtained at this scale.
What is my realistic chance of getting a full-time offer after the internship?+
Smatana does not have a quota that would either guarantee or limit a transition to full-time. The decision depends on the quality of collaboration, the volume of work in that period, and mutual satisfaction based on the ongoing reviews. Statistically, after a successful internship an offer typically comes in one of three forms (full-time, part-time, or external specialised collaboration) — not every Grant Coordinator wants full-time, and the studio is open to combined formats that fit the person's life situation.
Do I need previous experience with grants or EU programmes?+
No. Previous experience is a plus but not a requirement — the entire onboarding plan is set up so that the Grant Coordinator builds the necessary expertise during the internship under direct mentoring from the Creative Director, and from an external consultant on complex calls. More important than prior grant expertise is the ability to learn quickly, strong English, attention to detail, and the ability to work with structured processes.
Which AI tools will I use, and do I need to know them in advance?+
The main tools are Claude (including Cowork, Projects, Design), Notion, n8n or Make (for automation), Lovable or v0 (for quick prototypes), and possibly Cursor for custom scripts. Previous experience with these tools is a plus but not a requirement — during the first two months, the Grant Coordinator goes through practical onboarding with the whole stack, and the studio's admin team helps with the initial setup of all accounts, accesses and automations. More important than prior knowledge of specific tools is openness to AI as a working partner and a critical eye on AI outputs — the Grant Coordinator never submits an application that AI generated without their own review.
What if I want to use AI more on some sections and less on others?+
That is fine, and even desirable. The principle of the whole workflow is that AI replaces repetitive and monitoring tasks, while strategic, language-heavy and interpretive parts stay with the human. The exact AI vs. manual ratio is something the Grant Coordinator calibrates over time based on the type of task, output quality and personal preference — some people prefer AI as a first draft, others as a checking layer after a manual draft. Both approaches are legitimate.
How does the studio handle holidays, public holidays and breaks?+
The internship is designed so it can be aligned with exam periods, holidays and breaks. A few days before going on holiday, the Grant Coordinator informs the Creative Director, especially if an application deadline is coming up — at that point the work is either rescheduled, or some of the tasks are temporarily covered by the Creative Director or an external consultant. Outside of deadlines, time off is a matter of planning, not approval.
What do I get after successfully completing the 12-month internship?+
On completion, the Grant Coordinator receives an official certificate for the internship in both Slovak and English versions, listing the scope of work and the outcomes achieved. This comes with a personal reference from the Creative Director that names the specific applications prepared, the value of contracted funding and the key skills developed during the internship. At the same time, we discuss the possibility of continued collaboration — full-time, part-time or as external specialised cooperation. If the Grant Coordinator decides to leave the Smatana ecosystem, the studio actively helps with introductions to its European network — Creative Europe Desks, partner agencies in Berlin and Tallinn, co-production partners.
What if I make a mistake that puts an application at risk?+
Mistakes are a natural part of learning, and the whole application preparation system is designed to catch them before they reach the donor. Before every submission, there is a multi-stage check — the Grant Coordinator prepares the draft, an AI agent (Claude Cowork) runs a compliance audit on formal requirements, the Creative Director does the final content review, and on complex calls an external EU funding consultant is also involved. The final responsibility for submission is never carried by the Grant Coordinator alone. If, despite the multi-stage check, an application gets a technical reject or a deadline is missed, we deal with it together — we analyse what happened, what we could improve in the process, and we move on. The Smatana brief is not that the Grant Coordinator should never make mistakes — it is that the system as a whole becomes more robust with every new application.
